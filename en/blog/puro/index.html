Puro-2B: How Can a Poor Lab Pretrain a Model from Scratch?

Matching Qwen2-1.5B on RTX 5090 GPUs—with a $5,090 budget

What would it cost for a lab on a budget to scale up from small-model experiments to pretraining billion-parameter models on trillions of tokens?

Models in this size range are widely used, from Qwen2-1.5B and Qwen2.5-1.5B to Qwen3-1.7B. Qwen releases the weights, but not the full pretraining recipe. OLMo and SmolLM also release data and training details, yet reproducing them remains expensive: the estimated compute costs for OLMoE-1B/7B and SmolLM3-3B are about $200,000 and $719,000, respectively—even at this relatively modest scale. 1, 2

For a lab on a budget, knowing how to train a model is not enough—you also need to be able to afford it. We need a low-cost, reproducible pretraining recipe.

Puro-2B: A Fully Open Recipe for Pretraining on a Budget

To make this possible, we built Puro-2B and opened up the full training pipeline: training data, framework, configurations, and model weights.

We trained a roughly two-billion-parameter model from scratch on a cluster of consumer RTX 5090 GPUs, testing several training budgets and using up to 1.4 trillion tokens. With our recipe, $4,400 in pretraining compute is enough to match Qwen2-1.5B on the average score across 15 tasks. (The $5,090 in the headline is a nod to the GPU’s name—we actually spent less.) A run with the more complete recipe costs about $6,900 and comes close to Qwen2.5-1.5B on the same 15-task average. Compared with other open recipes, Puro-2B substantially lowers the cost of pretraining.

Switch model

What if your budget is smaller still? We fit an empirical cost–performance curve—the Puro Cost Scaling Law—to estimate how far different budgets can go. Starting from the same Phase 1 checkpoint, we run Phase 2 with randomly shuffled data (Uniform Data, or UD) at several training budgets, then fit average evaluation score against total cost. This curve is specific to the Puro recipe. Researchers can use it to estimate achievable performance and adapt the recipe to their needs before committing to a full run, without first repeating our longest training run.

The savings come from designing five parts of the training pipeline to work together: hardware choice, low-precision training, optimizer improvements, effective use of high-quality data, and data selection.

RTX 5090: More Compute per Dollar

Let’s start with the hardware. Which GPU gives you more compute for the same budget? We compare the cost per GPU-hour using H200 rental quotes and the cost of owning an RTX 5090, amortized over five years, plus electricity. The resulting RTX 5090 rate is close to the market rental quotes available to us. On this basis, the RTX 5090 delivers about 2.77 times as much peak BF16 compute per dollar as the H200. Its peak throughput is lower, but its GPU-hour cost is lower by an even larger factor, so the same budget buys more compute. Consumer GPUs are also easier for labs on a budget to acquire.

Improving GPU communication. There is a catch: the RTX 5090 has only 32 GB of memory and no NVLink, so we cannot simply reuse a training setup designed for data-center GPUs. By adjusting drivers and system settings, we enable PCIe P2P communication within each node, increasing one-way GPU-to-GPU bandwidth from 31.5 GB/s to 56 GB/s and cutting latency from 14.3 µs to 0.4 µs. Across nodes, GPUDirect RDMA raises the 24-GPU AllReduce bus bandwidth from 8.87 GB/s to 19.93 GB/s. 3, 4

Balancing compute and memory. We also tailor the training setup to billion-parameter models and the RTX 5090’s hardware constraints. We combine data parallelism with pipeline parallelism, avoiding communication-heavy tensor parallelism. We then rebalance work across pipeline stages, tune the microbatch size, and distribute optimizer states according to memory usage to balance memory load across nodes. Together, these changes bring overall model FLOPs utilization (MFU) above 70%, turning consumer GPUs’ price advantage into lower training costs.

2.77×Efficiency gains and supporting experiments+

H200 BF16 → RTX 5090 BF16

Which GPU gives you more BF16 compute for the same budget? The answer depends on both its compute throughput and how long the budget lets it run. The H200 provides 989.5 TFLOP/s of peak BF16 throughput at $4 per GPU-hour. The RTX 5090 provides 209.5 TFLOP/s at about $0.3061 per GPU-hour. The RTX 5090 rate comes from our hardware and electricity costs, about CNY 12,000 per month for an eight-GPU node.

For $1, the H200 can run for 1/4 = 0.25 hours, while the RTX 5090 can run for 1/0.3061 ≈ 3.27 hours. Multiplying each runtime by its peak throughput and taking the ratio gives (209.5 ÷ 0.3061) ÷ (989.5 ÷ 4) ≈ 2.77. The RTX 5090 has lower peak throughput per GPU, but its price is lower by an even larger factor. The same budget therefore buys about 2.77 times as much peak BF16 compute.

With cheaper compute in place, the next step is to make training faster with FP8.

FP8: Faster Training on RTX 5090 GPUs

The RTX 5090’s Blackwell architecture supports FP8 training. Following DeepSeek-V3’s fine-grained blockwise quantization approach, we run the main linear-layer matrix multiplications in FP8 and keep numerically sensitive operations and training states in BF16 or FP32. See the full FP8 training scheme for an overview of these precision choices. 5

Does the speedup outweigh the loss penalty? We compare FP8 with BF16 at five model sizes in a scaling ladder. FP8’s validation loss is about 0.003–0.004 higher, with a small, relatively stable gap across scales. At 1.7B parameters, however, FP8 delivers 1.36 times the training throughput of BF16. Faster throughput is not the whole story: we also account for the extra compute FP8 needs to reach the same loss as BF16. After this quality adjustment, the estimated net efficiency gain is still about 1.34×.

1.34×Efficiency gains and supporting experiments+

MuonH BF16 → MuonH blockwise FP8

FP8 runs faster but uses lower numerical precision. To estimate how much training time it saves, we consider both the number of tokens processed per second and the training compute needed to reach the same validation loss.

We first compare BF16 and blockwise FP8 at five model sizes. FP8’s validation loss is about 0.003–0.004 higher than BF16’s. Using the fitted loss–compute curves, we translate this gap into the extra compute needed to match the loss and find that FP8 still retains about 98% of the compute efficiency.

We then account for runtime throughput. On the 1.7B model, measured FP8 throughput is about 1.36 times that of BF16. Combining this throughput gain with the compute-efficiency factor gives a net gain of about 1.34 times, corresponding to an estimated 25.2% reduction in GPU-hours at the same validation loss.

MuonH: Rethinking Optimization Through Effective Learning Rates

Precision determines how we compute an update. The optimizer and learning rate (LR) determine its direction and size. We use MuonH, a variant of Muon that normalizes the update and projects the updated weights back onto a fixed-radius sphere. The effective learning rate (ELR) measures the update size relative to the weight norm. By controlling both norms, MuonH makes the LR for the matrix parameters it handles numerically equal to their ELR, measured before projection. Setting the LR schedule for these parameters therefore directly sets their ELR trajectory. 6

Can standard Muon reproduce MuonH’s loss curve if we match their ELRs? We test this with a 170M-parameter model trained in BF16. This control, Muon-ELR, does not project weights onto a fixed-radius sphere: their norms remain free to change. Instead, we adjust Muon’s scalar learning rate at each step so that its effective learning-rate trajectory follows MuonH’s. For the matrix parameters handled by MuonH, the ELR equals the prescribed learning rate, so we use that schedule as the target. Once the ELRs are aligned, the two loss curves nearly overlap. This suggests that, in this experiment, MuonH’s advantage is not explained by the norm constraint alone: the effective learning-rate trajectory is key to understanding why Hyperball optimization works. 6, 11

A closer comparison shows that when weight decay does not sufficiently constrain weight norms, standard Muon’s ELR drops more quickly early in training, and its loss falls sooner. MuonH shows a more gradual ELR decay and performs better later in training. This shifts the question from which optimizer to use to how to design its effective learning-rate schedule. 7

How long should the decay phase be? Using a Warmup-Stable-Decay (WSD) schedule, we vary the peak learning rate, training-token budget, and fraction of the run spent in decay. Higher peaks and longer runs generally benefit from a longer decay phase. These results motivate the long linear decay in Phase 2, giving the loss time to converge. Phase 1 uses a power schedule for a different reason: it lets training continue without a predetermined endpoint. 8

Comparing compute at matched loss. The ELR-alignment experiment asks whether standard Muon can reproduce MuonH’s loss curve. The next question is how much compute each recipe needs to reach the same validation loss. We compare five model sizes, from 0.17B to 1.7B parameters, in a scaling ladder, using blockwise FP8 for both recipes. The tuned Muon baseline here uses a learning rate chosen for each model size and decays it linearly. This is different from the Muon-ELR control above, which adjusts the scalar learning rate at every step to match MuonH’s ELR. Fitting loss–compute curves to these five pairs of runs suggests that the MuonH recipe is about 1.19 times as compute-efficient as the tuned Muon baseline.

Tuned Muon → MuonH (both blockwise FP8)

How much less compute does MuonH need than Muon to reach the same validation loss? We train models at the same set of sizes with each optimizer, use blockwise FP8 in both cases, and tune the Muon baseline. We then fit a loss–compute curve to each set of results.

At a given validation loss, we read off the FLOPs required by each curve. Muon needs about 1.19 times as much compute as MuonH. In other words, if Muon needs 100 units of compute, MuonH needs about 84, saving roughly 16.1%.

CMA: Getting More from High-Quality Data Late in Training

The optimizer is only part of the story. The order in which a model sees its data matters too. High-quality examples make up a small but important share of the training corpus, and placing them later can help the model make better use of them. Many open datasets include quality scores for individual samples. We use these scores to order examples from lower to higher quality within each source, then mix blocks from the same relative positions in these ordered sequences. As training progresses, the model sees higher-quality examples while the mix of sources stays roughly constant within each local training window. This avoids abrupt shifts in domain proportions as data quality changes.

Placing high-quality data late in training also changes what we need from the learning-rate schedule. Our earlier work found that when the learning rate becomes too small late in training, even high-quality examples have little effect on the model’s parameters. We address this with Curriculum Model Averaging (CMA). We first train Phase 2 with linear learning-rate decay, then resume from the checkpoint at step 218,000. From there, we hold the learning rate at its value at that step rather than decaying it further, allowing the model to keep learning from the remaining high-quality data. Finally, we take the arithmetic mean of corresponding parameters across the last six checkpoints of this continued run. The averaged parameters become the final model, reducing the influence of fluctuations in any single checkpoint. 9

Extra-Merge is another recent advance in model merging during pretraining: it extrapolates along merged-checkpoint trajectories without additional gradient updates. 12 Our CMA recipe uses equal-weight checkpoint averaging, without extrapolation.

How much does data order matter? With the data pool and learning-rate decay schedule held fixed, curriculum ordering outperforms random shuffling. CD decay final scores 57.17, versus 55.99 for UD decay final. Averaging checkpoints along each decay trajectory gives 57.18 for CD decay avg and 55.57 for UD decay avg. The curriculum improves the average score by 1.18 and 1.61 percentage points in these two comparisons, showing that changing the data order alone can help. 9

Averaging along the decay trajectory brings little benefit. The UD average score falls from 55.99 to 55.57, while the CD score changes from 57.17 to 57.18. Simply averaging late checkpoints while keeping the learning-rate decay does not produce a meaningful gain.

Averaging helps after constant-LR continuation. For the run resumed at step 215,000, averaging the last six checkpoints raises the score from 55.64 for CDC 215k final to 56.80 for CMA 215k avg. For the run resumed at step 218,000, averaging raises the score from 57.12 to 57.81. These gains of 1.16 and 0.69 percentage points suggest that averaging is more effective when the model continues learning from high-quality data at a constant learning rate late in training. Our final CMA model follows this recipe: use curriculum-ordered data, resume from step 218,000 at that checkpoint’s learning rate, keep it constant, and average parameters across the last six continuation checkpoints.

Choosing Training Data with Small-Scale Experiments

Once the data order is settled, we still need to decide which data belong in the mix.

We sample a subset from each candidate data source. For large datasets with quality scores, we draw separate samples from different score ranges. For other eligible sources, we sample at random. Each subset is used to continue training from the same model checkpoint under the same configuration. We then evaluate every run on the same math, code, Chinese, and general benchmarks. 10

These proxy experiments reveal differences both between data sources and within a single source. They also expose trade-offs: sources that yield stronger code scores tend to yield lower scores on general benchmarks, while the trade-off between math and general performance is weaker. We therefore give math data a slightly larger share than code in Phase 2, then refine the mixture based on domain coverage and quality filtering. The small-scale results help us decide which sources and score ranges to keep, and how much weight to give each. We publish the dataset evaluation results in the report.

Beyond Cheaper Training: Studying the Full Pipeline

Hardware makes compute cheaper, and FP8 makes training faster. MuonH reduces the compute needed to reach the same validation loss, while CMA helps the model make better use of its training data. Proxy experiments guide data selection. Together, these choices form our low-cost pretraining pipeline.

Control pretraining, then compare post-training outcomes. With only released weights, researchers have little control over how a model was pretrained. Puro-2B lets them change the pretraining recipe on a smaller budget, then hold the fine-tuning and evaluation procedures fixed to measure the effect on the final model.

Do CMA’s gains survive fine-tuning? We fine-tune CMA- and Uniform-pretrained models using the same procedures. In two math fine-tuning experiments, starting from CMA improves GSM8K accuracy by 1.77 and 2.02 percentage points. With Tulu-3 mixed-domain fine-tuning, the 15-benchmark average improves by 1.17 percentage points and IFEval by 1.36 percentage points. All three comparisons show overall gains, suggesting that CMA’s pretraining benefits carry over under these fine-tuning setups. 9
